The Problem

Same data. Two completely different realities.

KNOC's legacy ESH (Environment, Safety & Health) system was being replaced as part of a Digital Transformation initiative. On paper, the problem was clear: the old system was inefficient. In practice, the problem turned out to be more specific.

Headquarters

  • Couldn't verify data accuracy from branches
  • No standardized input formats meant data was unreliable
  • Unable to detect process errors in real time
  • Data entry and extraction felt wasteful

9 Branch Offices

  • Worked directly with raw, unprocessed data
  • No clear data entry standards — reports were subjective
  • Repeatedly rebuilding the same field reports manually
  • Reports from different authors created communication gaps

The insight that changed the brief: Initial interviews surfaced generic DT complaints. But through Affinity Diagram analysis, a structural pattern emerged — HQ and branches weren't just using the same system differently. They needed fundamentally different things from it. That reframe drove every design decision after.

Research & Key Decisions

How I decided what to build

1

4 weeks of targeted interviews — not generic discovery

Weekly Zoom sessions with 25 participants: 20 users from 9 branch offices, 5 from headquarters. Sessions were structured to surface real workflow friction, not wish lists. Photos from on-site sessions at branch offices provided context that remote interviews couldn't.

The initial findings were common DT complaints — not particularly useful on their own.

2

Affinity Diagram: redefining who the users actually were

Running an Affinity Diagram on the raw interview data produced an unexpected result. When treated as one user group, the data was noisy. When separated into HQ and branch perspectives, two distinct problem spaces emerged with different root causes and different success criteria.

This led to a deliberate decision: build one product with two modes, not one product trying to serve everyone the same way.

3

Competitive audit before wireframing

Before designing anything, I audited dashboard patterns from competitors in centralized control systems. The goal was to understand what made enterprise dashboards scannable under real work conditions — not just aesthetically clean.

Key finding: information hierarchy mattered more than information density. Users found it easier to process data when the most critical indicator was centered, not buried.

4

Defining the right data attributes for 10 menus

For the branch dashboard, the challenge wasn't visualization style — it was deciding what to visualize. Each of the 10 safety management menus had different operational logic. I analyzed the core attributes of each menu to determine which data points were genuinely actionable vs. informational.

Example: "Contractor Management" needed a purchase count. "Risk Index Overview" needed risk scores plus conditions. The graph type followed from the data type — not the other way around.

5

Concept testing with all 9 branches

The most significant finding from branch concept testing: graph effectiveness varied sharply by task relevance. The same visualization that worked well for one team's workflow was irrelevant for another's. This validated the need for personalized, task-specific dashboard configurations — not a one-size-fits-all layout.

Solution

One dashboard, two distinct modes

The solution was a dual-architecture dashboard: a centralized control view for headquarters and a personalized, task-oriented view for branch offices. The two modes shared the same underlying data but were structured around fundamentally different user goals.

Feature 01 — Branch Offices

Personalized data visualization across 10 management menus

Each of the 10 safety management menus was given a dedicated visualization built around its core attributes. Branch users could remove low-priority dashboard cards and navigate directly to relevant task boards via a "+more" button — reducing the cognitive load of monitoring 10 different domains at once.

More than half of users in concept testing validated the need for dashboard features customized to their specific team's main tasks.

Feature 02 — Headquarters

Centralized visibility dashboard with standardized data inputs

The HQ dashboard was designed to maximize visibility across all 9 branches simultaneously. A/B testing between left-aligned and center-aligned layouts showed users found center-aligned critical indicators significantly easier to scan — a finding that directly influenced the final layout structure.

The map-based overview gave headquarters real-time status visibility across the organization, with drill-down access to individual branch data.

Reflection

What I learned, and what I'd change

What this project taught me

The most important design decision wasn't visual — it was the choice to split one user group into two. That reframing happened during research analysis, not during design, which reinforced how much the quality of synthesis matters in enterprise UX. When the problem space is complex, the framing of the problem is the design work.

What I'd do differently

The project ended at mid-fidelity. I'd push to establish clearer success metrics before development — specifically, task completion rates by branch type and HQ data verification time — so post-launch performance could be measured against the original problem statement, not just stakeholder satisfaction.

Next step identified: Onboarding service design for KNOC employees adapting to the new system — addressing the change management side of DT that pure dashboard design doesn't solve.
